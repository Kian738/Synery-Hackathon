# 04 Landmark-based, multisensory guidance: research and design rules

Purpose: how the scenario results (and ordinary routes) should be *spoken and shown* so people can follow them, using landmarks without inventing any. This is research for a design, not a validated emergency-guidance system. Nothing here is an instruction for trained responders or emergency procedure.

## What the evidence supports (and how strongly)

Only claims whose source was opened this session are marked **verified**; the rest are carried from `reports/Indoor wayfinding agent UX.md` and flagged.

| Finding | Source | Strength and limits |
|---|---|---|
| People remember turns and decision points better than straight-walking intersections and assume "keep going straight" when unsure. In a familiar-city study participants omitted about 60% of straight intersections but only about 23% of turns. | Meilinger, Frankenstein and Bülthoff, "When in doubt follow your nose", Frontiers in Psychology 5, 26 Nov 2014. **Verified (abstract and summary).** | Familiar city, virtual model, route *planning*; consistent with working-memory limits. Supports: give instructions at decision points and mode changes only, fold straight runs into the next turn. |
| Route directions rated as better contained more landmark, segment and turn mentions; good directions for familiar and unfamiliar routes differ in the *types* of landmarks. | Lovelace, Hegarty and Montello, "Elements of good route directions in familiar and unfamiliar environments", COSIT '99, LNCS 1661, pp. 65-82 (1999). **Verified (abstract level, plus a secondary review for the landmark correlation; the paper's own tables were not read).** | Outdoor, human-written directions rated by people. Correlational. Supports landmarks as an element of quality; does not say how many. |
| Navigation instructions augmented with landmark-related information improved later spatial learning: route retracing without assistance, landmark recognition and sketch maps, with no cost to driving performance. | Gramann, Hoepner and Karrer-Gauss, Frontiers in Psychology, 13 Feb 2017, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00193 (open access, PMC5303713). **Verified (abstract).** | Driving simulator with GPS-style speech; the helpful details included *personally relevant* landmark information, and the "irrelevant detail" group also improved. Evidence about **recall and recognition after the trip**, not about speed of following during a stressful event. |
| A route description length of about four segments was best for recall and usefulness indoors; longer, more verbose directions are harder to recall. | Nourin, Pramanick and Sarkar, "How much is too much", arXiv 2408.15367, 27 Aug 2024. **Verified (abstract: "an ideal length of four route segments").** | Preprint, small indoor study. The repo's UX report cites a 4-segment (85%) versus 6-8 segment (65-67%) success comparison from the paper body; **not re-checked here** and the report itself notes the difference was not significant. |
| Salience of a landmark can be scored (visual, semantic, structural; plus visibility on approach). | Raubal and Winter 2002, via `reports/Indoor wayfinding agent UX.md`. **Not re-fetched.** | The repo already implements a salience score in `floorwise/core/instructions.py` (size, uniqueness within 15 m, closeness to the door, seen from the doorway; categories `machine` and `equipment` only; minimum salience 0.35). |
| One prominent landmark per instruction was more efficient than four; short keyword-style voice responses were rated as useful as full sentences. | Bauer et al. (via Pharos) and Haas et al. CHI 2022, via the UX report. **Not re-fetched.** | Treat as design hints, not settled. |
| Colour must not be the only way information is conveyed, and instructions must not rely solely on sensory characteristics (shape, colour, sound, location). | WCAG 2.2, W3C Recommendation, document dated 12 December 2024 on w3.org/TR/WCAG22/, success criteria 1.4.1 Use of Color and 1.3.3 Sensory Characteristics. **Verified (criterion wording trimmed in the tool output).** | A web-accessibility standard, applied here by analogy to wayfinding on screen and in speech. It is not a wayfinding or fire standard. |
| Standards that cover accessible evacuation exist (for example ISO 21542:2021, whose scope includes egress and fire evacuation and which has a clause on wayfinding). | Scope only, from catalogue pages. **Clause text not read; no requirement is claimed from it.** | Read the standard before citing any multi-sensory requirement. |

Not found in what was read: direct experimental evidence that *landmark cues improve evacuation speed or safety*. The evidence above is about route recall, direction quality and spatial learning in normal navigation. Claims about emergencies would be extrapolation and must not be made.

## Design rules for the product (each tied to the evidence above or marked as a design choice)

1. **Instructions at decision points only** (Meilinger 2014); straight passes merge into the next step; a floor change is its own step. *(Already the behaviour of `instructions.py`.)*
2. **At most four spoken parts per overview, then step mode** (Nourin 2024, UX report). Each part is one action plus at most one landmark, around 15 words. Longer routes continue in step mode with "next / repeat / back".
3. **Landmarks come only from the supplied data.** The candidates are the building's own named entities: rooms (name and number), assets (type and name, currently `machine`/`equipment` categories), openings (door, entrance, opening; width), lifts and stairs, floor names. If no candidate clears the salience threshold the step has **no** landmark and says "the door" or the room name; it never invents one ("the red door", "the painting") and never promotes a generic asset to a distinctive one. Because the data's footprints and items are *illustrative* in places (see `docs/STATUS.md` on stairs and the asset catalogue), speak a landmark only if its type is certain from the data, and phrase it by type ("the coffee machine"), not by appearance.
4. **No distracting stories or filler.** The Gramann 2017 result shows that added landmark-related detail can improve later recall; it does not justify narration during guidance. Keep to one landmark; any "recognition cue" is optional and short (rule 7).
5. **Never rely on one sense.** Every step exists as (a) text, (b) an icon, (c) speech, and (d) optional haptic on the phone; none is the only carrier (WCAG 1.4.1 and 1.3.3 by analogy). Colour (route amber, blocked red, accessible green in DESIGN.md) is always paired with words and an icon. Speech is always mirrored as text for noisy or silent contexts.
6. **Counts only for small numbers** ("the second door", up to about three) and **metres rounded** ("about 20 metres"); blind or low-vision users get step counts, not metres, per the UX report's sources (not re-fetched).
7. **Optional repeat and recognition cues (design choices, to be tested, not proven):**
   - *Repeat*: the same words again, never a paraphrase, so a listener can compare (the phone companion's Repeat and the voice command "repeat" already do this).
   - *Look-ahead recognition cue*: one short line before a decision point, "You will pass the coffee machine, then turn left", at most once per step and only if the landmark qualifies (rule 3). Evidence base: recognition and recall benefits in Gramann 2017 and landmark mentions in Lovelace 1999; the effect on following speed is unmeasured here.
   - *Confirm cue* on arrival at the landmark ("The coffee machine is on your right, turn left here") may be offered in step mode only; it doubles as a "you are on route" check without claiming the system knows where the person is (the app has no positioning; progress is confirmed by the person, never inferred from the phone's sensors).
8. **Say what is assumed.** Scenario guidance always states which closures and capacities the route assumes ("assuming Lift 2 is closed and the east corridor is blocked, as you told me").
9. **Hazard-aware wording is out of scope.** The system must not tell anyone to move through or avoid smoke, fire or heat, or give evacuation orders. In a scenario result it describes *modelled routes under stated assumptions*, with the anti-claim sentence from `02-demo-api-evidence.md` section 6.

## How this plugs into the scenario results

Each route row in a scenario result (`rows` of `02-demo-api-evidence.md` section 5) carries the existing `Step` structure from `instructions.py` (kind, icon, text, short spoken form, metres, floor, landmark). The voice output uses the `short` strings and the four-part rule; the screen shows the full step list. No new landmark logic is needed for a first version: it reuses the existing salience scoring and the "one landmark or none" rule. A test already implied by the rules: every spoken landmark string must correspond to an asset, room or opening id in the building, and a step with no qualifying landmark must carry `landmark: null`.

## Open questions for the user (not blocking)

- Should the optional recognition cue be on by default in step mode, or opt-in? (Design choice: opt-in until measured.)
- Is there a person or group with real evacuation-route familiarity data to test the cues against? Without it, the cues stay unvalidated design.
